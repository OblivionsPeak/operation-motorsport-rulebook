§ 01

Mission & Core Values

1.1 Our Mission

Operation Motorsport is committed to providing disabled veterans with meaningful recovery opportunities through virtual motorsports. Our eMotorsports program creates a supportive environment where beneficiaries can experience camaraderie, rebuild connections, and find purpose through the shared passion of racing.

1.2 Why Two Classes

This series runs prototypes and GT cars on the same track at the same time. That is a deliberate choice, not a cosmetic one. A two-class field gives every driver something to work on that isn't lap time:

  • It creates a ladder. A BEN driver has somewhere to go. The LMP2 seat is earned, and the path to it is written down in §3.
  • It teaches awareness. Traffic management is the most transferable skill in sportscar racing, and the only way to learn it is in traffic.
  • It puts mentors on track. PRO and PRO BEN drivers share the circuit with the drivers they mentor, every single lap, and are judged on how they handle it.
  • Everyone races someone. A driver having a difficult day in GT3 is still racing the GT3 field, regardless of what the prototypes are doing.

1.3 The Beneficiary-First Approach

Every race, event, and interaction should contribute to an enjoyable, supportive experience that promotes veteran recovery.

  • Fun First: Every race, event, and interaction should contribute to an enjoyable experience for our beneficiaries.
  • Accessibility: Qualified beneficiaries receive their initial 3-month iRacing subscription — available to new accounts only — and all required track and car content at no cost (limited to 2 cars and the tracks on the league calendar, reimbursed after the season, and the driver must compete in no less than 75% of the entire season).
  • Supportive Environment: We foster an atmosphere of encouragement where all skill levels are welcomed.
  • Community Building: Through Discord and live-streamed events, we create opportunities for meaningful connections.
  • Recognition: We celebrate our beneficiaries' dedication, strength, and perseverance on and off the track.

1.4 Community & Race Ethos Agreement

Every driver must commit to these six principles before participating in any race session:

  1. Competition comes second to community.
  2. Celebrate the wins with other drivers, always.
  3. Discuss failures in an academic environment with no blame or pressure.
  4. Always keep our eyes forward to the next race; we keep our demons in the rear view.
  5. Every driver finishes as a team, or we all lose as a team.
  6. Most importantly: have FUN.

1.5 Order of Precedence

Where two instructions conflict, the higher authority governs:

  1. This rulebook.
  2. Any published Bulletin issued for the season after this rulebook.
  3. The Event Supplement for a specific round — track, time of day, weather, pit window.
  4. The Race Director's pre-race briefing and in-session instructions from Race Control.

A briefing instruction beats the book for that race. If Race Control tells you something in the briefing that contradicts a rule here, the briefing wins for that event only, and the conflict gets fixed in a Bulletin afterward.

§ 02

Community Code of Conduct

2.1 Inclusive Environment

Operation Motorsport is an inclusive racing community dedicated to supporting veteran recovery. We welcome drivers of all skill levels and backgrounds. Discussion of politics, religion, and other controversial topics is prohibited in official channels. Incidents of racism, discrimination, or hate speech will not be tolerated and will result in immediate removal from the program.

2.2 Respect & Sportsmanship

Treat everyone with respect. This community is built on mutual support and encouragement. Drivers are expected to be courteous, humble, and open-minded.

  • No toxic behavior, including excessive negativity, personal attacks, or harassment.
  • No calling out other drivers, staff, or mentors in public channels — let a driver call themselves out.
  • Address concerns privately through proper channels or designated feedback sessions.
  • Negative voice or text chat during races is prohibited.

2.3 Communication Guidelines

Keep radio chatter to a minimum during races so the channel stays clear for race-related information — which, in a two-class field, includes traffic calls that keep people out of the wall. If frustration arises from an incident, take a breath and wait until after the race to discuss it calmly.

  • In-game text chat is not to be used during qualifying or the race, except by Race Control.
  • Radio transmissions must be plainly understandable. No codes, no ciphers, no deliberately misleading calls.
  • A working microphone is required. Traffic in this series moves faster than your mirrors.

2.4 Incident Reporting

Use the designated Discord ticketing system to report race incidents. Include relevant details, timestamps, and if possible, replay footage. Focus on what happened, not who is to blame. The goal is learning and improvement, not punishment. Full procedure and deadlines are in §12.

Unclassified drivers are treated as PRO until a class is formally assigned. This removes any incentive to delay or avoid classification.

3.2 Classification & Class Adjustment

Class assignment is made by Program Management before the season and reviewed as the season runs. The league reserves the right to move a driver between classes, in either direction, to keep the racing fair and the beneficiary field healthy.

  • A driver is entitled to two completed races in a class before any move is made.
  • With few exceptions, no driver is moved after Round 4, so everyone finishes the season fighting in the same class they spent it in.
  • A driver who is moved carries their championship points into a fresh tally in the new class; the previous class standings are settled without them.
  • Licence points carry across a move, reduced by 1 point if the driver holds 2 or more at the time of the move.

3.3 Earning the Prototype Seat

Moving from BEN to PRO BEN means moving from GT3 to LMP2. That is a real step up in speed and responsibility, so it is a considered decision rather than an automatic one. A BEN driver becomes eligible when they meet either performance route:

  • Rating route: 1500+ iRating.
  • Season route: a completed full BEN season with at least one podium finish.

In addition, the league may promote a BEN driver on pace alone where they are clearly beyond the class: race-average lap times within approximately 103% of the GT3 class best, sustained across two events, together with a clean incident record over the same period.

Promotion is never a punishment and is never automatic on pace alone. Before any driver moves up, Program Management will discuss it with them. A beneficiary who is eligible for PRO BEN may elect to remain in BEN. Nobody is forced out of the class where they are enjoying their racing.

Rookie Protection

A driver in their first season with the program is never moved up mid-season on pace alone. They finish the season in the class they started, unless they ask to move.

3.4 PRO and PRO BEN Responsibilities

PRO and PRO BEN drivers serve as mentors and ambassadors for the program. In this series they also drive the fastest car on track, which means they carry the greater share of responsibility every time the two classes meet.

  • Provide guidance on simulator setup and racecraft when requested.
  • Race with awareness that beneficiary experience takes priority over personal results.
  • Model exemplary sportsmanship and patience on track — most of all in traffic.
  • Accept that the prototype driver is held to the stricter standard when the classes make contact. See §7.2 and §12.3.

3.5 OPEN Class Expectations

OPEN class drivers are guests in our program, and they share the GT3 field with the beneficiaries this series is built for. They must understand and embrace the beneficiary-first approach. OPEN drivers who create an overly competitive environment that negatively impacts beneficiary engagement may have their participation reviewed.

Where an OPEN driver's pace is disruptive to the BEN field rather than additive to it, the league may move that driver to LMP2 or decline their entry for subsequent rounds. This is a fit decision, not a disciplinary one.

3.6 Entry, Attendance & Withdrawal

  • Drivers are not required to enter every round. Sign-ups and sign-outs are handled per round through the league's registration channel.
  • Drivers who miss two rounds without signing out may be withdrawn from the series entry list. Signing out costs nothing and keeps your entry.
  • New entries received from Round 5 onward are placed in the standings but are not eligible for the championship, so a late arrival cannot disturb a title fight.
  • No new entries are accepted for the final round.

4.2 Equipment Requirements

  • Active iRacing subscription, and ownership of the required car and track content for each round.
  • Working microphone for race communications — required, not optional, in a two-class field.
  • Steering wheel and pedals. Controllers may not be used for steering.
  • A stable internet connection.

4.3 Driving Assists

In-game driving assists are permitted for accessibility purposes, without limit and without needing to justify them. Many of our drivers race with assists because of injury, and that is exactly what they are for.

Where a driver's goal is to develop, we encourage gradually reducing ABS and traction control and turning off the racing line as skills develop — at the driver's own pace, on the driver's own timetable. No one will be asked to give up an assist they rely on.

4.4 Third-Party Software

  • Permitted: overlays and telemetry tools that display information the sim already provides — iOverlay, RaceLabs, TrackTitan, Garage 61, VRS and similar.
  • Prohibited: any software that modifies the simulation, provides inputs, or produces a performance advantage the sim does not otherwise offer.

In a two-class field, a spotter or radar overlay is a safety tool. We would rather you ran one.

4.5 Connection Issues

Drivers experiencing connection problems (blinking, warping, or dropped position reporting) may be asked to pit and address the issue. If the driver does not enter the pits at Race Control's request in a timely fashion, they may be force-pitted or removed from the session without recourse. Persistent connection issues affecting others may require the driver to withdraw for that round.

Technical issues on the driver's side — game crashes, internet disruption, hardware failure — do not warrant a restart, a replay, or points relief. These are beyond the league's control.

4.6 Paint Schemes & Numbers

  • Custom liveries are welcome and encouraged.
  • No offensive, political, or activist-themed schemes.
  • Schemes should reflect the supportive spirit of the program.
  • Car numbers are assigned for the season and held by the driver. LMP2 numbers are single or double digit; GT3 numbers are double or triple digit, so class is readable at a glance on a timing screen.
§ 05

Event Format

5.1 Session Schedule

SessionLengthNotes
Practice30 minOpen to both classes. Setup work, track familiarisation, mentorship.
Qualifying15 minLone qualifying — each driver in their own instance. See 5.3.
Start1 lapPace car leads the field to the green flag. See §6.
Race60 minOne mandatory pit stop. Pit window 15:00–45:00. See §9.

All drivers are expected to be ready to race at the designated time for official sessions, and to remain in the pits for the entirety of the driver briefing. Only Race Control decides whether a session is delayed or restarted.

5.2 Practice

  • Practice is shared between the classes on purpose. It is the cheapest place to learn what a closing prototype looks like in your mirrors.
  • PRO and PRO BEN drivers are asked to spend part of practice running in traffic rather than chasing a lap time alone.
  • Be courteous. Do not use practice to make a point about someone's driving.

5.3 Qualifying

Qualifying uses a lone qualifying format. All drivers qualify during the same 15-minute session, each alone in their own instance, so nobody ever has to set a lap with another car anywhere near them. This removes traffic entirely, which matters more in a two-class field than in any other format — there is no closing prototype in your mirrors and no cold GT car on your line.

Each class sets its own grid and its own pole from the times recorded in that session. See 5.4 for how the two classes are then ordered on the grid.

  • Drivers may complete as many flying laps as the session time allows. Your best valid lap counts.
  • Drivers are required to qualify to the best of their ability — a genuine, good-faith effort to set the fastest time they can, given their skill, equipment, and conditions.
  • A driver may not deliberately start from the back. If you want a last-to-first run, start from the pit lane instead.
  • If a driver is still learning and cannot complete a valid lap, they may start from the back of their class with no penalty and no comment. This is expected and entirely acceptable.
  • Late arrivals may forfeit their qualifying position and start from the back of their class, or from the pit lane after the first lap.

5.4 Grid Formation

The grid is drawn up in a staggered two-column formation and ordered by class: all LMP2 ahead of all GT3, each class in the order of its own qualifying times. Overall pole is therefore always an LMP2. The field runs to the green double file — see 6.1.

This is the single most valuable structural rule we take from IMSA. It means the prototypes never have to fight their way through the GT field in the opening laps, which is where multi-class racing goes wrong most often.

  • A driver who sets no time qualifies at the back of their own class.
  • Cars sent to the back of a class are ordered first-come, first-served, with sporting penalties placed behind elective changes.
  • Where a car is absent at the pace lap, the grid closes up by position.

5.5 Track & Weather Conditions

Practice, qualifying, and race conditions should remain broadly consistent through an event, and are published in the Event Supplement for each round. Time of day and weather may vary by round, including dusk and night events later in the season, but they will not change unannounced within an event.

§ 06

The Start

6.1 The Pace Lap

The pace car leads the field to the green flag. The field completes one pace lap behind it, double file, in assigned grid order.

  1. The field forms up in two columns behind the pace car in grid order — all LMP2, then all GT3. The pole sitter takes the pole side; every car behind takes the side opposite the car ahead of it.
  2. Hold your column and your position. No changing sides and no change of order at any point on the pace lap.
  3. Tire and brake warming is permitted on the early part of the lap. Riding the brakes is acceptable at any time.
  4. From the final corner onward, no weaving, no abrupt acceleration or braking, and no tire scrubbing. Close up to a consistent gap and hold it.
  5. A car that falls out of the formation must not fight to regain its position. It starts from the back of its own class.
  6. The pace car leaves the racing surface on its final approach. Hold pace-car speed until the overall leader accelerates.

Lining up on the wrong side is a false start, and it is attributed to the first car in the order that took the wrong side — not to the cars that then had nowhere to go.

6.2 The Green Flag

  • The overall leader controls the start and may accelerate once the pace car has left the racing surface and the leader reaches the start zone.
  • Hold pace-car speed until the leader accelerates. Do not accelerate before the car in front of you.
  • Maintain your lane and do not overtake within your column until you have individually crossed the start/finish line.
  • If the car in second reaches the line ahead of the pole sitter while respecting the above, that is not a false start.
  • If Race Control waves off the start, hold formation, reduce pace, and expect the green on the next crossing.
Lap One Is Fully Stewarded

Every incident on lap one of every race is reviewed, whether or not anyone reports it. Lap-one incidents also carry an uplifted penalty. Nothing good has ever come from winning a race on lap one.

6.3 Jump Starts & False Starts

A false start is any deviation from the start procedure, your assigned position, or the briefing before the green is given. Gamesmanship, brake checking, and manipulation of the pace are prohibited. A missed shift or wheelspin is not a false start.

If you gain a position by jumping the start, give it back before completing the lap and no further action will normally be taken.

6.4 Pit Lane Starts

  • A car unable to reach the grid, or still in the pit lane when the pits close for the pace lap, starts from the pit lane.
  • Pit lane starters join only when released, after the field has passed pit exit on the first racing lap.
  • A car first driven to pit exit after the leader has already completed a lap may join without penalty.
§ 07

Multi-Class Racing

This is the section that makes or breaks a two-class series. Read it twice. An LMP2 is several seconds a lap faster than a GT3, which means the prototypes will lap the GT field repeatedly in a 60-minute race, and every one of those passes is a chance for someone's evening to end early.

7.1 The Governing Principle

The Multi-Class Rule

The faster car is the responsible car.

The two sanctioning bodies we draw on disagree on this, and we have picked a side. IMSA holds the overtaking and the overtaken driver mutually responsible, and treats the blue flag as advisory only — a GT driver under blues has no obligation to concede at all. That works in professional sportscar racing because every car has a spotter, a race engineer, and a driver who has done it for years.

SRO takes the other view. Its GT World Challenge regulations carry an explicit Pro/Am fault presumption: in an incident between categories, the more professional driver is presumed responsible unless there is clear evidence otherwise, and a Pro-on-Am incident draws a heavier penalty than the reverse.

We follow SRO, for the obvious reason. We have beneficiaries learning the craft in GT3 and experienced drivers in prototypes. So responsibility in this series is asymmetric and it points at the prototype — see §12.3 for how that is applied — and the blue flag is a command, not a suggestion.

7.2 Duties in Traffic

LMP2 — The Overtaking Car
  • The pass is yours to complete safely. If it goes wrong, the presumption is against you.
  • Choose the place. Pass on a straight or in a clearly open braking zone — never by diving down the inside of a GT that has already turned in.
  • Close the gap, then look. Do not arrive at a corner already committed to a pass you have not set up.
  • Assume the GT has not seen you until it shows you it has.
  • Signal your intent with a headlight flash — no more than three flashes.
  • If the pass is not on, back out. You will get another lap. The GT driver may not get another race.
  • Never force a three-wide situation on a GT car.
  • Leave a full car's width. A GT that is squeezed to the kerb becomes a passenger.
GT3 — The Car Being Lapped
  • Be predictable. Predictability is the whole job. Hold your line and let them come to you.
  • Do not lift or brake mid-corner to be helpful. That is how prototypes end up in the back of GT cars.
  • Concede on the straight, not in the corner. Ease off the throttle, stay off the racing line, and leave one clear side.
  • Check your mirrors and relative timing on the straights, where it is safe to look.
  • You are not obliged to concede to a car that is lapping you, but do not actively defend against it either. You are not racing it.
  • Do not move off your line unpredictably to "get out of the way" — that is more dangerous than holding it.
  • You keep the right to race the cars in your own class, always. See 7.4.

7.3 Blue Flags

A blue flag means a faster car is closing to lap you. Following IMSA practice, in this series it is an advisory flag — it is information, not an instruction.

The blue flag tells you a prototype is coming. It does not oblige you to surrender the position, and there is no lap count within which you must concede. Completing the pass safely remains the responsibility of both drivers.

  • You are not required to concede on a blue flag, and you will not be penalised for failing to do so.
  • You are still expected to be predictable. Hold your line and let the faster car come to you. Predictability, not courtesy, is what keeps this safe.
  • Where you choose to concede, do it on a straight or a wide, open corner exit — never mid-corner, and never by lifting or braking unexpectedly.
  • You keep the right to race the cars in your own class at all times. See 7.4.
  • Un-lapping is permitted. You may re-pass a car that has lapped you.

Advisory does not mean anything goes. The following remain prohibited and are penalised under §8.3 and §11.2 regardless of the blue flag:

  • Deliberately impeding or blocking a faster car — defending a position you are not racing for.
  • Brake-testing, weaving, or reactive blocking against a lapping car.
  • Any move made to make the pass harder rather than to drive your own race.

7.4 Racing Your Own Class

Nothing in §7 takes away your right to race the drivers in your own class. A GT3 driver in a class battle is racing, and a prototype arriving behind that battle must wait for it, not through it.

  • An LMP2 driver may not use their closing speed to break up a GT3 class fight.
  • Where a GT3 car is defending a class position, the prototype takes the wide line and waits for the straight.
  • GT3 drivers in a class battle are encouraged to acknowledge blues at the first safe point once the battle resolves.

7.5 Closing Speed & Awareness

  • Prototype drivers must expect that a GT car may not know they are there. Drive for the mistake you can see coming.
  • GT drivers must expect that a prototype closes far faster than it looks in a mirror. A car that appears comfortably distant on the exit of a corner can be alongside at the next braking zone.
  • Both classes: use the straights for awareness, the corners for driving.
Minimum Pace

Drivers should maintain a race lap time at least equal to 115% of the best lap set by the fastest car in their own class. A car consistently slower than this, or unable to hold a predictable line, may be asked by Race Control to pit or withdraw for the safety of the field.

§ 08

On-Track Regulations

The Golden Rule

Race people the way you want to be raced.

Quality racecraft is expected at all times. This is not a win-at-all-costs environment. §7 governs when the two classes meet; this section governs racing generally, including racing within your own class.

8.1 The Right to the Line

The driver in front has the right to choose any line at any section of the track. That right is lost when overlap is established. Overlap exists when:

  1. On a straight — the overtaking car brings any part of its car alongside any part of the car in front.
  2. Inside pass — the overtaking car brings its front end up to at least the middle of the lead car, under control, before the lead car turns in. This may be achieved under braking, in a straight line.
  3. Outside pass — the overtaking car brings its front end up to at least the middle of the lead car, under control, by at least the apex.
  4. Mistake — the driver in front misjudges the entry, runs wide, and misses the apex.

Once any of these is satisfied, the cars are side by side and both drivers owe each other racing room. A pass is complete at the moment there is zero overlap between the rear of the passing car and the front of the defending car.

8.2 Racing Room

  • Where cars are side by side, both must leave at least one car's width to the edge of the track surface.
  • From turn-in to the apex, the car on the outside determines how much room to leave the inside car — subject to always leaving a car's width.
  • From apex to exit, the car on the inside determines how much room to leave the outside car — subject to always leaving a car's width to the track edge.
  • Squeezing a car against a wall, kerb, or off the track surface is prohibited.

8.3 Defending

  • On a straight the lead car has one safe move, plus one further safe move before the braking zone. Choose your side early.
  • No moving over in reaction to an overtaking car when it is too late to avoid contact.
  • No weaving to break a slipstream or to block.
  • Before entering a braking zone, be committed to your line, and do not deviate from it while defending.
  • Where an attacking car has moved to the inside, the defender may react and hold the defensive line or return to the racing line — but must not close the door once overlap exists.
  • Reactive blocking, brake checking, and any deliberate wheel input under braking are prohibited whether or not contact results.

Transitioning from one overlap state to another — a straight into an inside pass — is where most avoidable contact happens. Closing the door in that transition is strongly discouraged and will be penalised where it is judged deliberate.

8.4 Prohibited Manoeuvres

  • Dive-bombing. If you have not earned the corner under braking, it is too late. The gap you can see is closing, you are in a blind spot, and the incident will be yours.
  • Movement under braking. Enter braking zones straight and parallel to the track.
  • Bump-drafting and bump-passing. Bump-drafting is prohibited outright in this series.
  • Punting. Using the car in front as a braking aid.

8.5 Track Limits

A car is on track as long as at least two wheels remain in contact with the track surface, which includes the integrated red-and-white kerbing.

  • Track limits are enforced by iRacing's own warning and penalty system in the first instance.
  • Going off with all four wheels through a genuine mistake is part of racing. Repeatedly leaving the track at the same corner may be judged as gaining an advantage and penalised by the stewards regardless of the sim's count.
  • Gaining a position or an advantage by leaving the track is prohibited. Give the position back, or slow to give up the advantage within the same lap, and no further action will normally follow.
  • Track-limit counts reset at the start of each session and do not carry between sessions.

Exemptions. A track-limits excursion is not counted against you where:

  • You were forced off by another car.
  • You went off avoiding an incident, or an off-track car rejoining.
  • You gained no advantage while making or defending an overtake.
  • You were off under yellow, or following a Race Control instruction.

Pit entry and exit lanes count as part of the track surface for cars on track. Drivers using the pits must stay within the pit lane lines with all four wheels; riding the line is acceptable.

8.6 Spins, Offs & Rejoining

  1. Hold your brakes. If you spin or lose control, hold the brakes so your car behaves predictably. Do not roll forward or backward into traffic. Failing to hold your brakes is a separate penalisable act from the incident itself.
  2. Wait. Rejoining is your responsibility. Wait for a genuine gap — that may mean waiting for the entire field, including a class you are not racing.
  3. Merge, do not cut. Rejoin parallel to the racing line, at the shallowest angle you can, never straight across it.
  4. Gain nothing. A car off the track must rejoin safely, gain no advantage, and not interfere with a car properly on track.
  5. Get towed if you are broken. If severely damaged, request a tow rather than blocking the track or limping round.

If you continue with severe damage — a meatball flag, no steering, a flat — and cause a further incident, you may be penalised for it. Where an incident is followed by a second one after you had time to regain control, the stewards will treat them as two separate incidents.

8.7 Lights & Signalling

  • Headlight flashes are permitted to signal an intention to pass, or to acknowledge a car giving way. No more than three flashes.
  • Prototype drivers are encouraged to use lights in traffic. It is the clearest signal available to a GT driver whose mirrors are full.
  • Flashing lights is never a demand and never a substitute for a safe pass.

8.8 After the Checkered

  • A car taking the checkered flag in the pit lane rather than on the racing surface is not credited with completing that lap.
  • After the flag, slow safely and either return to the pits or exit the session.
  • Contact after the race is forbidden and is penalised as severely as contact during it.

Minimum stop timePublished per round in the Event Supplement, measured pit-in loop to pit-out loop
Tires and fuelFree. No mandatory tire change, no minimum fuel

Failure to complete the mandatory stop within the window is a post-race time penalty equal to the round's published drive-through equivalent, plus 30 seconds. Failing to stop at all is a disqualification from the race result.

Deliberately keeping the window wide — 30 of the 60 minutes — is a beneficiary-first choice. It means a driver never has to nail a two-lap window under pressure, and a driver who misses their planned lap has plenty of road left.

9.2 Minimum Stop Time & Short Stops

Each round publishes a minimum total pit stop time, measured from the pit-in timing loop to the pit-out timing loop. Stopping short of it is penalised proportionately:

ShortfallPenalty
Under 2.00 s10× the shortfall, added to race time (short by 0.53 s → 5.3 s)
2.00 s or moreDrive-through, or its published time equivalent if the race ends first

This is taken directly from SRO's practice in GT World Challenge America, and it is far fairer than a flat penalty — a driver who is a tenth short pays a tenth's worth, not a race.

9.3 Pit Lane Conduct

  • Always engage the pit limiter. Drivers taking advantage by not engaging it will be penalised severely where the sim does not catch it automatically.
  • There is a minimum pit lane speed as well as a maximum. Proceed at or near the limit. Deliberately burning time in the pit lane to manipulate a minimum stop time or a pit window is prohibited.
  • Observe the fast lane and transition lane. Do not overtake using the transition lane.
  • When leaving your box, yield to cars already in the fast lane, accelerate in the transition lane, and only join the fast lane at or near the limit.
  • A car in the fast lane entirely behind a car in the transition lane must let it merge.
  • Do not cross directly from the fast lane into your box. Slow in the transition lane first.
  • Reverse gear is prohibited in the pit lane.
  • Stay within the pit lane lines with all four wheels. Do not cross the blend line at pit exit.
  • On exit, cars on track have right of way. Stay low, hold your line, and merge without forcing anyone off line.
  • Announce pit entry and exit on radio where you can — it is genuinely useful to a prototype behind you.
Pit Exit In A Two-Class Field

Pit exit is the single most dangerous place in a multi-class race. A cold GT3 joining at pit-lane speed and an LMP2 arriving on a hot lap is the classic season-ending accident. Exiting drivers stay low and predictable; drivers on track give the merging car room. Neither of you has to win pit exit.

9.4 Quick Repair & Damage

  • iRacing's Quick Repair may be used freely and does not satisfy the mandatory stop unless the car also stops for service within the window.
  • A car shown a meatball flag must pit for repair. Stewards cannot see your meatball in a replay, but they can see a steering wheel badly out of alignment, and will assume one.
  • A driver who continues with severe damage and causes an incident may be penalised for it.

10.1 Flag Reference

10.2 Yellow Flags

  • No passing under yellow. The prohibition starts at the point of the first displayed yellow and ends at the first displayed green.
  • Slow down and be ready to stop. A yellow is for your safety, and continuing at full pace until you can see the incident is how a single incident becomes five.
  • Extra caution is required near any incident with a stationary car, regardless of where you are relative to a flag. Failing to use caution can be penalised even where you were technically outside the flag zone.
  • If you are involved in an incident, hold your brakes until it is safe to move.
  • Do not improve your position under yellow. If you gain one, give it back.

10.3 Full Course Cautions

Full course cautions run on iRacing's native system, with the pace car and the sim's own pit-lane and passing enforcement.

  1. At the caution, slow immediately, stop passing, and close up on the car ahead. Overtaking is prohibited at every point on the track simultaneously.
  2. The pit lane closes at the moment the caution is called and reopens when Race Control announces it. Watch for the announcement rather than guessing.
  3. The field forms up single file behind the pace car.
  4. Cars unable to hold pace-car speed must move aside and let the field by.
  5. Race Control will call the pit lane open. The pit lane opens for LMP2 first, then GT3 on the following lap, then all cars. This keeps the two classes from arriving in the pit lane together.
  6. The restart is single file, led by the overall leader, who holds pace-car speed to the restart point before accelerating.
  7. Hold position and stay packed up until the leader accelerates and the green is shown. Manipulating the pace on a restart is a false start.
Why We Do Not Run IMSA's Category Split

IMSA re-sorts the field into class order before every restart — prototypes move right and pass the GT cars, GT cars move left and fall back. It is the best multi-class restart procedure in the world, and we cannot use it: iRacing automatically penalises passing under yellow, so drivers following the procedure correctly would be penalised by the sim for doing so. We therefore restart in running order, and accept that prototypes may have GT cars ahead of them at a restart. Give them room; you have 60 minutes.

10.4 Red Flags

Race Control may red flag a session for server instability or a blocking incident.

  • Follow the instructions issued by Race Control in chat and on Discord.
  • If less than 50% of the race distance has been completed, the race is restarted at its full original length.
  • If 50% or more has been completed, the race is not restarted. The result is taken from the last lap completed by the leader, with full points awarded.
  • Outstanding penalties from that lap and earlier are still applied to the result.

10.5 Race Control Authority

  • Race Control may direct any car to pit, may force-pit a car, and may remove a car from the session on safety grounds.
  • Race Control decisions on race procedure — starts, restarts, caution procedure, pit lane status, timing and scoring — are final and not appealable. Incident and conduct penalties may be appealed under §12.9.
  • Only Race Control decides whether a session is delayed, restarted, or abandoned.

Making procedural calls final is borrowed from IMSA's "Conclusive" concept. It exists to stop a stewarding team from spending its evening re-litigating pit-lane timing, and to keep the review effort where it matters — driving standards.

§ 11

Prohibited Conduct

11.1 Zero Tolerance

The following result in immediate review and may lead to removal from the program:

  • Intentional wrecking or retaliation of any kind.
  • Deliberate actions to bring out a caution or manipulate race results.
  • Harassment, abuse, or threatening behavior toward any participant.
  • Racism, discrimination, or hate speech.
  • Driving with malicious intent toward another competitor.

11.2 Other Prohibited Actions

  • Overly aggressive driving that creates an unwelcoming environment for beneficiaries.
  • Dive-bombing or slide-job passing that causes contact.
  • Bump-drafting, and any bump-draft that spins another car.
  • Excessive blocking that unfairly impedes competitors.
  • Using closing speed to break up a class battle you are not part of.
  • Third-party software that provides an unfair performance advantage — see §4.4.
  • Rage-quitting or disconnecting after causing an incident.
  • Racing under the influence to the point where it affects the safety of the field.

On the last point: we are a veteran recovery program and we are not going to be prudish about a beer on a Tuesday. But if a driver is causing repeated incidents and we become aware that impairment is involved, we will have a conversation about it — as a welfare matter first, and a penalty matter second.

§ 12

Incident Review & Penalties

12.1 Philosophy

Our incident review process is designed for learning and improvement, not punishment. We believe in honest self-assessment and supporting each other's growth. Accidents happen; what matters is how we respond and what we learn.

The machinery below exists so that outcomes are predictable rather than harsh. A driver should be able to read this section and know roughly what to expect before the stewards say a word.

12.2 Incident Severity

Every reviewed incident is placed in one of five categories. Severity is judged by consequence — what actually happened to the other car — not by how it looked.

CategoryDefinitionLicence pts
Racing IncidentNo single driver wholly or predominantly at fault, or the result of minor misjudgement or mitigating circumstances.0
No Further ActionOne car at fault, but the offending driver punished themselves or gained nothing.0.5
MinorAvoidable incident costing minimal time and/or 1–2 positions.1
ModerateAvoidable incident costing 2 or more positions and/or moderate time.2
SevereAvoidable incident causing major damage or significant time or position loss to a driver at no fault of their own.4

An incident is any occurrence or series of manoeuvres involving one or more drivers that results in an unfair advantage or disadvantage to another competitor, whether or not there was contact. A track cut or an unsafe rejoin is an incident even if nobody touched.

12.3 The Inter-Class Presumption

Adopted from SRO — GT World Challenge

Where the two classes collide, the more experienced category is presumed at fault.

SRO's GT World Challenge regulations carry an explicit Pro/Am fault presumption: in an incident between categories, the more professional driver is presumed responsible absent clear evidence to the contrary, and a Pro-on-Am incident carries a heavier penalty than the reverse. We adopt it directly, mapped onto our own classes:

IncidentAdjustment
LMP2 at fault on GT3One severity level up. A Minor becomes a Moderate.
GT3 at fault on LMP2One severity level down. A Moderate becomes a Minor.
PRO at fault on a BEN driverReviewed by a Head Steward regardless of severity.
BEN driver's first at-fault inter-class incident of the seasonCoaching, no licence points, no time penalty.
Same classNo adjustment.

This is not a licence for GT3 drivers to drive carelessly — the presumption is rebuttable, and a GT3 driver who brake-tests a prototype or ignores blues will be penalised normally. It reflects the plain fact that the driver with the speed, the experience, and the better view carries more of the duty.

12.4 Time Penalties

Lap-one incidents carry an uplift, because lap one is where the damage multiplies. Penalties are applied the same way whether or not the driver reported the incident themselves.

SeverityLap 1Lap 2 onward
Minor15 s10 s
Moderate20 s15 s
Severe30 s25 s
  • In-session penalties must be served under green, and must be concluded before the checkered to count as served.
  • You have four green-flag crossings of the start/finish line to serve a penalty before it escalates.
  • A penalty issued too late to serve is converted to a post-race time penalty.
  • A driver who receives an automatic drive-through for accumulated warnings, and is then forced off by another driver, may report that. The other driver may be penalised — but the drive-through stands.

12.5 Licence Points

Licence points accumulate across the season and roll for 12 months. Thresholds differ by class, and they are deliberately strictest for PRO — the drivers with the most experience and the fastest car are held to the highest standard, not the lowest.

  • Thresholds stack and are served worst-first. A driver reaching the back-of-grid threshold serves that, then any lower penalty at the following event.
  • Points decay. Complete an event with no at-fault incident and no more than 2 iRacing incident points from contact you caused, and 1 licence point is removed. A driver who cleans up their driving climbs back out.
  • Points may be suspended. At the stewards' discretion, points may be held in suspension and cancelled entirely if the driver completes three subsequent events cleanly. This is the preferred outcome for a first-time offender.
  • Any driver reaching the Race DSQ or Series Review threshold must meet with a Head Steward or the Chief Steward for a review conversation before racing again.
  • Reaching a threshold is never the end of anyone's participation on its own. It triggers a conversation, not an exit.

12.7 The Stewarding Process

  • Reviews are conducted by a minimum of three stewards.
  • A Driver Advisor — an experienced driver from the field — may attend to advise on driving standards. They contribute perspective but hold no vote. Adopted from SRO's GT World Challenge structure.
  • A steward may not review an incident they reported, or one involving their own car, and must recuse themselves.
  • Stewards may adjust a defined penalty by one severity level in either direction. A Head Steward may adjust by two. Anything beyond that requires two Head Stewards or the Chief Steward.
  • Stewards exercise judgement on light contact, taps, and bumps — a lap-one nudge that happened to cost three positions is treated differently from the same nudge on lap twenty.
  • While reviewing a reported incident, stewards may review anything else they see in it, including a failure to hold brakes or the origin of a pile-up.
  • Drivers with recurring incidents are subject to increased penalties regardless of the circumstances of the immediate incident.
  • The target is to complete stewarding within 48 hours of the incident log closing.

12.8 Reporting

  • Wait one hour after the race before filing a report. This is a cooling-off period, and it exists because reports written angry are worse reports.
  • You have 24 hours to file a report.
  • Reports must follow the format in the report channel. Incomplete reports are voided.
  • You may report incidents involving your own car, and any genuine safety concern you witnessed directly.
  • You may not file reports about blocking or impeding, and may not request blue flags on another car. Race Control handles that live. This rule is adopted from SRO and exists purely to keep the stewards' time on driving standards.
  • Lap one of every race is fully stewarded whether or not anyone reports it.

12.9 Appeals

  • Drivers may appeal an incident or conduct penalty within 24 hours of notification, through the Discord ticketing system, with a clear explanation of why the ruling should be reconsidered.
  • Race procedure decisions are final and may not be appealed — starts, restarts, caution procedure, pit lane status, timing and scoring, and short-stop calculations. See §10.5.
  • Onboard footage that becomes available after the review is not, on its own, grounds to reopen a decision the stewards could properly make without it.
  • A penalty applied to the wrong car should be reported immediately and will be corrected without needing an appeal.

13.1 Scoring Philosophy

The scoring system rewards participation, clean racing, and finishing over pure speed — reinforcing that competition comes second to community. Points are scored by position within your own driver class, so a BEN driver's championship is decided against other BEN drivers and nobody else.

13.4 Classification

  • All cars that start the race are credited with a finishing position, whether or not they are running at the checkered.
  • You do not need to take the checkered flag on track to be classified and score points.
  • To be listed as running, you must cross the line on the racing surface when the checkered is shown. A car crossing in the pit lane is not credited with that lap.
  • Cars are ordered by whole laps completed, then by the time they crossed the line.
  • Post-race penalties that change the order elevate every other affected car.

13.5 Success Time (Optional)

The league may enable Success Time for a season or a portion of one, to keep a class competitive where one driver is dominating. It is off by default and will only ever be introduced between seasons, never mid-season.

Success Time is applied to pit stop time rather than as ballast, following SRO's practice in its one-make series — it is exact, it is visible, and it does not change how the car drives.

13.6 Immersion Event Points

Operation Motorsport places beneficiaries with real-world race teams at live events, and those events will sometimes fall on a race night. A beneficiary who misses a round because they are attending a real-world race event with a team scores the points for the position that team finished in that event.

  • The team’s finishing position in its own class is read across to the points table in 13.2 and awarded to the driver in their own driver class. Where the real-world event runs multiple classes, the class result is used, not the overall result — a team finishing 2nd in class and 14th overall scores as 2nd.
  • Where the team does not finish, the driver scores the DNF points in 13.2, read from the distance the team completed — 2 points at 50% or more of the event distance, 1 point below it. A team’s mechanical failure costs the driver no more than their own would have.
  • These points are awarded in addition to the points scored by the drivers who raced, and displace nobody. A round may therefore show two drivers on a winning score.
  • The round counts as one of the driver’s scoring rounds under Best 6 of 8, and counts as an attended round under 3.6.
  • Bonus points under 13.3 are not available for an immersion round — there is no lap time and no incident record to judge.
  • The driver signs out of the round in advance through the registration channel and confirms the event with Program Management, who apply the points once the real-world result is official.
Why This Exists

A beneficiary standing in a real paddock on a race weekend is the program working exactly as intended. Nobody should have to weigh that against their championship.

§ 14

Championship

14.1 Season Structure

Eight rounds. Best 6 of 8 results count toward the championship, allowing two drop races for life and scheduling conflicts.

The season calendar is published before the season opens and is fixed once set. No round may be added, removed, rescheduled, or have its circuit changed without the approval of the Program Manager.

Drivers buy content and plan their lives around the calendar. A fixed calendar is worth more to this field than the freedom to change it.

14.2 Titles

Four driver-class championships, decided within class:

  • BEN Champion — GT3
  • OPEN Champion — GT3
  • PRO BEN Champion — LMP2
  • PRO Champion — LMP2

And two car-class championships, combining the driver classes that share a car:

  • GT3 Champion — BEN and OPEN combined
  • LMP2 Champion — PRO BEN and PRO combined

14.3 Tiebreakers

  1. Most first places in class, then most seconds, then most thirds, and so on.
  2. If still tied, the driver who reached their highest finishing position earliest in the season.
  3. If still tied, most Clean Race bonuses.

14.4 Season Awards

🎖️
Mentor of the Season

The PRO or PRO BEN driver voted by the beneficiaries.

📈
Most Improved Driver

The driver with the greatest iRating improvement during the season.

❤️
Community Spirit Award

Voted — embodies all six of the community ethos principles.

🛡️
Cleanest Driver

Most Clean Race bonuses across the season, in either class.

14.5 Rule Changes & Feedback

  • Unless a change is unanimously agreed, we will do our best to never implement mid-season rule changes.
  • Post-race reviews and guided open practice sessions run through the week. They are not required, but they are where most of the learning happens.
  • A survey goes out at the end of every season. If you want something changed, that is the place. All feedback is welcome, positive or negative.
  • Where we agree something needs fixing but not how, we will put it to a driver vote.

§ 16

Final Thoughts

Operation Motorsport exists to support veteran recovery through the power of motorsports. Every rule in this document serves that mission. Racing should be challenging and exciting, but never at the expense of our beneficiaries' experience or wellbeing.

This rulebook is longer than the one before it, because two classes on one track need more written down than one class does. But none of the added structure changes what the series is for. The prototype seat exists to give a beneficiary something to aim at. The traffic rules exist so that reaching for it is safe. The fault presumption in §12.3 exists because the person with the most experience should carry the most responsibility — on a race track and everywhere else.

We are not a professional racing league. We are a community of drivers who come together to support each other, have fun, and share our passion for motorsports. Trophies and championships are secondary to the relationships we build and the healing that happens when we race together.

Always Remember

Competition comes second to community.

Questions, concerns, or suggestions should be directed to the eMotorsports Program Management.
